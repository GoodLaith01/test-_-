What is happening

The simulation starts at 0.2 Hz, one full cycle every 5 seconds.

STAGE 1 OF 4

Step down the voltage

Mains current in the primary coil makes an alternating magnetic flux in the iron core. The flux induces a smaller voltage in the secondary. The frequency does not change.

Vs / Vp = Ns / Np = 12 V / 230 V
Vpeak = √2 × Vrms = √2 × 12 ≈ 17 V
STAGE 2 OF 4

Rectify with diodes

A silicon diode conducts only when its anode is about 0.7 V above its cathode. Half-wave uses one diode and throws away the negative half. The bridge steers both halves through the load in the same direction.

Half-wave: Vout,pk = Vp − 0.7 V
Bridge: Vout,pk = Vp − 1.4 V ≈ 15.6 V
STAGE 3 OF 4

Smooth with a capacitor

Near each peak the diodes conduct briefly and charge the capacitor. Between peaks every diode is reverse-biased and the capacitor alone feeds the load, so the voltage sags a little. That sag is the ripple.

Vr(p-p) ≈ IL / (fr · C)
fr = f (half-wave), 2f (bridge)
STAGE 4 OF 4

Why frequency matters

With 100 Ω and 1000 µF the time constant RC is 0.1 s. At 0.2 Hz the bridge recharges every 2.5 s, so the capacitor empties long before the next peak. At 50 Hz it recharges every 10 ms and the output is almost flat.

50 Hz: Vr ≈ 0.156 A / (100 Hz × 1000 µF) ≈ 1.6 V
Design rule: RC ≫ 1 / fr

Try this

  1. At the slow start, follow one amber dot from terminal A through D1, the lamp and D3 back to B. Then follow a cyan dot through D2 and D4. Both flow the same way through the lamp.
  2. Remove the capacitor. The lamp now flashes twice per cycle, and the scope shows pulsating DC that touches zero.
  3. Switch to half-wave. D2 and D4 are taken out and D3 becomes a plain wire. The lamp flashes once per cycle, and the ripple doubles.
  4. Let the sweep reach 50 Hz, then set playback to 1/50 ×. You can watch short charging pulses at each peak while the capacitor carries the load in between.
  5. At 50 Hz, change C and R. A larger C or a larger R (smaller current) gives less ripple, as the formula predicts.
  6. Switch to electron flow. Electrons move opposite to conventional current, against each diode arrow.
